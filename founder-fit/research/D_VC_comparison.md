# D. VC comparison of four candidate companies for Elliot Greenlaw

Agent F, seed/Series A investor lens. Date: 2026-10-01. Only the founder profile was read; the other files in this folder were not.

## Founder in one paragraph (as an investor sees him)
- A student who ships iOS apps alone and has strong design taste.
- Some direct trades exposure through Greenturf, no licences, no capital, part-time.
- Based in Oklahoma, with a faith-driven "trust over pressure" brand.
- Past apps (OTD, Friendly Fire, Locus) show he ships. None shows distribution or paying customers.
- Pre-seed risk is therefore **distribution and sales**, not product. The best idea for him is the one where he can get to dozens of paying customers without a sales team, regulators, or a multi-sided cold start.

## C1. Roof Record (verified roof records and a "roof passport")

| Dimension | View |
|---|---|
| Market realism | The TAM story (about 100M roofs, insurers pay for roof age and condition) is real, but the paying buyers are insurers, who already buy aerial data from EagleView, Nearmap, Verisk and Cotality. The roofer-side SaaS market is small. Oklahoma's roof-age bill dying in May 2026 shows the policy tailwind is weaker than assumed, and insurers' appetite for third-party roof records is unproven. |
| Founder fit | Medium. iPhone/LiDAR and design are real strengths, and Oklahoma is a hail-belt home market. But he has no roofer relationships, no insurer relationships and no licences, and the product needs both. |
| Capital before first revenue | Low to build, but revenue is distant. Roofers will not pay for something that gives them no job-winning ROI. Realistically 18+ months to meaningful revenue, funded by free usage. |
| Margin | Software margins on the roofer side. The data-licensing side could be very high margin if it works. |
| Defensibility | Potentially strong later (a proprietary, verified, photo-backed dataset), and zero early. It is a cold-start network. Competitors can copy the app, and CompanyCam already holds the roofer's photo workflow. |
| Exits and acquirers | Verisk, Cotality, EagleView, CompanyCam, Nearmap, insurers' data arms, Roofr/AccuLynx/JobNimbus. |
| EV in success case | Most likely a data or tuck-in acquisition at $20–60M. Upside to $100M+ only if insurers pay for the data at scale. Comps are CompanyCam (outlier) and data-asset deals at 4–8x. |
| P($10M ARR) | **About 2–3%.** Two-sided cold start, a buyer who says "interesting" without paying, and a stalled policy catalyst. |
| 90-day gate for a $500k cheque | (a) 15 or more roofers using it unprompted for 3 or more jobs each. (b) At least 2 homeowners asking for the passport at sale or insurance time. (c) One insurer or agency willing to sign a paid pilot or LOI for the data. Without (c) it is a photo app. |

## C2. Instant Quote (satellite-measured, owner-priced, bookable quotes for exterior trades)

| Dimension | View |
|---|---|
| Market realism | Good. Many small lawn, fence, pressure-wash and roofing companies lose leads because they quote slowly. Customers want a price now. A realistic reachable base is a few hundred thousand small US exterior-service businesses. At $100–200 a month, $10M ARR is about 4,000–8,000 customers, or fewer with payments. That is achievable but demands steady distribution. |
| Founder fit | **Highest of the four.** He has landscaping experience through Greenturf, premium front-end and iOS skills, and a "price honesty, no pressure" brand that matches the instant-quote customer experience. He can build the product and run a sales demo himself. |
| Capital before first revenue | Lowest. Measurement APIs (Google Solar, Nearmap or similar) cost money per call, but a lawn pilot can go live in about 8 weeks on under $50k. First revenue should come within about 4 months. |
| Margin | Software at about 70–80% gross margin. API and measurement costs per quote take 5–10 points. Payments take rate at 2.5–3% (about 0.5–1% net after interchange) lifts revenue per customer but lowers blended margin. |
| Defensibility | Weak at the start. Jobber, Housecall Pro, LMN, Roofr and others offer quoting or instant estimates, and lawn marketplaces (LawnStarter, Lawn Love) already own part of it. Moat has to come from local pricing data, conversion benchmarks and workflow lock-in, which takes time. Churn among tiny operators is high (monthly churn of 3%+ is typical). |
| Exits and acquirers | Jobber, ServiceTitan (its SMB pushes), Housecall Pro, LMN, Granum/Service Autopilot, Angi, Thryv, Porch, payments players. Tuck-in acquisitions of quoting features are common. |
| EV in success case | $10M ARR with about 40% payments mix: 5–8x revenue gives **$50–80M**. Without payments, 4–6x gives $40–60M. Premium vertical-SaaS multiples need growth of 40%+ and net revenue retention above 100%. |
| P($10M ARR) | **About 6–8%.** The highest here because distribution can be tested cheaply and he can iterate fast. The main risk is crowding and churn. |
| 90-day gate | (a) 25 paying lawn companies at $50 or more a month, or 100 or more with a live quote link on their site. (b) Quote-to-booking conversion above their current rate, measured. (c) Under 4% monthly churn in the cohort. (d) A repeatable acquisition channel (local Facebook groups, equipment dealers, or franchise networks) with CAC under 3 months of gross profit. |

## C3. School-choice money rails (tuition billing and reconciliation for ESA, tax-credit and ECCA money)

| Dimension | View |
|---|---|
| Market realism | Real and growing: state ESA programs plus ECCA from 1 January 2027 (27 states opted in so far). Small private and microschools struggle to reconcile multiple scholarship streams. But FACTS (Nelnet), Smart Tuition, Blackbaud, ClassWallet and the SGOs themselves already own billing and disbursement. The prize is the long tail they ignore, and that tail is a lot of tiny customers with very low ACV (maybe $1–3k a year). $10M ARR needs several thousand schools or a payments take rate on large flows. |
| Founder fit | Medium. The Christian school network is a trust channel he could plausibly reach (Oklahoma Christian ties are a hint, not proof). But the problem is money movement, compliance (money transmission, state program rules, audit trails), and relationships with state agencies and SGOs. He is not a lawyer or CPA, and he cannot easily hire them. |
| Capital before first revenue | Medium to high. Needs a payments partner (Stripe Connect or similar), compliance counsel, SOC 2 or its equivalent for school data, and probably state-by-state integration work. Sales cycles follow the school-year calendar, so a missed August launch costs a year. Call it $250–500k to reach meaningful revenue. |
| Margin | Software 75%+. Payments take-rate at about 0.5–1% net after costs. Strong if flows are large, but reconciliation and support are labour-heavy. |
| Defensibility | Medium to high once embedded. Money flows, state-specific rules and reconciliation history are sticky, and regulatory complexity is a moat against generalist SaaS. Weak against Nelnet or an SGO bundling the same feature. |
| Exits and acquirers | Nelnet/FACTS, Blackbaud, Ascend, Finalsite, ClassWallet, Stripe-adjacent payments players, Fiserv, Brightwheel (adjacent), large SGOs. FACTS itself sold for only about $62M in 2005–06, so a niche winner in this space is a $50–150M outcome, not a unicorn. |
| EV in success case | $10M revenue with embedded payments: 6–9x gives **$60–90M**. Education software tends to trade below general vertical SaaS because school budgets are slow. Brightwheel (about $735M reported) shows a breakout is possible, but it was early-childhood with huge scale. |
| P($10M ARR) | **About 4–6%.** Real demand and a good timing story, but compliance and sales-cycle load on a solo student founder is heavy, and the ECCA opt-in and program rules may shift. |
| 90-day gate | (a) 10 or more schools signed (paid or LOI) in one state. (b) One SGO or state-program administrator as a design partner. (c) A payments partner and counsel confirmed that the money-flow design does not require him to hold a money transmitter licence. (d) A written view of how it beats FACTS for a 40-student microschool. |

## C4. OTD Check (buyer's-order audit sold to credit unions as a member benefit)

| Dimension | View |
|---|---|
| Market realism | Narrow. About 14–16M new cars and many more used cars are sold each year, but the consumer will not pay much for an audit, so the buyer is the credit union. Credit unions pay for funded loans, and a few hundred dollars per funded loan is plausible. $10M ARR means tens of thousands of incremental funded loans a year. TrueCar's take-private at $227M after years of public-market life shows how hard this category is. |
| Founder fit | Medium to low. His OTD app is a direct precursor, and the "honest to the buyer" brand fits. But the actual job is selling to credit union executives and compliance teams (vendor diligence, 9–18 month cycles), where a student has no credibility. |
| Capital before first revenue | Low to build, but long runway to first CU contract. Consumer-direct revenue could come first but is small. |
| Margin | High on software. Revenue is lumpy and depends on loan funding conversion, which the CU controls. |
| Defensibility | Weak. A buyer's-order parser is easy to copy. The moat would be CU integrations and funded-loan attribution. CarEdge, TrueCar, CUDL/AutoSmart-style programs and dealer-side tools already sit near this. Dealers may also fight it. |
| Exits and acquirers | CU service organisations and indirect-lending platforms, TrueCar/Fair Holdings, CarEdge, Carvana-like players, auto-finance fintechs. |
| EV in success case | Marketplace or lead-gen style revenue gets 1.5–4x. $10M of revenue gives **$20–40M**. TrueCar's price is the cautionary comp. |
| P($10M ARR) | **About 2–3%.** |
| 90-day gate | (a) One credit union signed to a paid or free-to-start pilot. (b) 50 audited buyer's orders showing real savings (average above $400) and that buyers actually sent the loan to the CU. (c) A funded-loan attribution method the CU's lending team accepts. |

## Ranking (for this founder)
1. **C2 Instant Quote.** Back it, at pre-seed.
2. **C3 School-choice money rails.** Do not fund now. Revisit in 6 months, only if he has schools signed and a clean legal structure.
3. **C4 OTD Check.** Pass.
4. **C1 Roof Record.** Pass.

## Plain verdicts
- **Back C2.** It has the shortest path to real revenue, the lowest capital need, and the best fit with what he can do alone (design-led product, iOS and web, trades exposure). It is also the only one where the first 25 customers do not depend on a regulator, a credit union committee or an insurer. The economics are ordinary and not venture-grade on their own, so the payments and trade-OS expansion is what makes the investment case, and I would hold the cheque until he shows he can sell. Expect a $50–80M outcome in a good case. That is an attractive result for a $500k cheque but not a fund-returner.
- **Pass on C1.** Two-sided cold start, no paying buyer for the data, and the Oklahoma bill's death removed a catalyst. Nothing in 90 days would be conclusive unless an insurer commits money.
- **Pass on C4.** The buyer is hard to reach, the sales cycle is long, the product is easy to copy, and the closest public comp (TrueCar) shows a low ceiling.
- **C3 is the best long-run business but the wrong one for this founder now.** Real regulatory tailwind, but he lacks the licences, capital and sales muscle, and a payments mistake in a faith-based customer base would break the trust his brand depends on. If he insists, I would want him to start as a pure reconciliation tool (no money movement) for one state's microschools.

## Single question for the first meeting
"Name the first 25 people who will pay you, tell me how each one will hear about you, and what you did last week to get one of them to say yes."
