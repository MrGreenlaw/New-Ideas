Redbud. A founder-fit venture plan for Elliot Greenlaw, prepared on the first of October, twenty twenty-six.

This is the full plan, written to be heard rather than read. It runs a little under an hour at a relaxed pace. It begins with how the idea was found, then describes the company itself, and then walks through the numbers, the risks, and exactly what to do over the next thirty days, the next hundred days, and the next three years. It ends with the strongest case for the company, the strongest case against it, and the evidence that is still missing.

Let us start with how we got here.

The task was to find a company that you, Elliot, are unusually well placed to start and win, and that could grow very large. Founder fit was treated as seriously as market size. So the search did not stop at apps. It looked at software for the trades, at phone-based scanning and three-dimensional printing, at consumer trust and faith-based ventures, at structural shifts in Oklahoma such as storms, insurance and energy, and at a deliberately contrarian sweep of problems across the whole economy.

Twenty-five independent analysts worked through four rounds. Some estimated markets. Some studied competitors. Some played the customer. One played a venture capital investor who saw only your profile and a one-paragraph description of each idea. And in every round, one analyst had a single job: to prove the idea should not exist. Together they produced about sixty candidate ideas.

Here is the honest summary of what happened to them. Nearly every idea that fit your app-building and design skills was small, crowded, or had nobody willing to pay. A digital roof record for homeowners died because no one would pay for it before a storm. Instant satellite quotes for lawn and fence companies turned out to be a feature that half a dozen companies already sell. Software for private schools handling school-choice money died in Oklahoma, because here the tax credit is paid to parents, so schools have very little to reconcile. A car-buying audit sold to credit unions died because credit unions depend on dealers for more than half of their auto loans and would not pay for a tool that audits those same dealers. A pipeline placing trade-school graduates into jobs died because Oklahoma's career-tech centres already place nearly all of their graduates for free.

But one thread kept surviving. Every time an idea touched roofs and storms, the analysts found that the real money and the real pain were not in roofing software at all. They were in the homeowner's insurance bill. Oklahoma homeowners pay about five thousand three hundred and seventy-eight dollars a year on average for home insurance, the fourth highest in the country. Across eight hail-belt states, homeowners pay about thirty-six point six billion dollars a year in premiums. And one analyst, who was never told that insurance was under consideration and was asked only to find ideas with a proven billion-dollar comparable, independently ranked a trust-first Oklahoma insurance agency as the single best bet for you.

Just as important is what the research disproved, because several favourite assumptions fell along the way. Oklahoma's proposed law limiting non-renewals for older roofs, and limiting decisions based only on aerial photographs, passed the House but died in the Senate on the fourteenth of May, twenty twenty-six. The Fortified roof discount, which seemed like a possible advantage, turns out to be required of every admitted insurer by Oklahoma law, so it gives no agency an edge. There is no published evidence that Fortified roofs perform better against hail specifically; the strong study is about hurricane wind in Alabama. And Oklahoma law already stops insurers from non-renewing or surcharging someone just because of a first claim or a weather claim, which weakens the idea that people switch insurers right after a hailstorm. The plan you are about to hear is what remained after those cuts.

Now, the company.

The working name is Redbud. The redbud is Oklahoma's state tree, and it blooms in early spring, right as storm season begins. The name has not yet been cleared for trademark or web address.

Redbud is an honest, licensed, independent home and auto insurance agency for storm country, starting in Edmond and Oklahoma City. In one sentence: Redbud reads your home insurance in plain dollars, re-shops it every renewal, and tells you the truth, even when the truth is, stay where you are.

Here is how it works. A family photographs their insurance policy summary, the page insurers call the declarations page, along with their renewal notice. Redbud's software pulls out the important numbers. A licensed agent checks every one of them, and then the family receives a one-page explanation in plain dollars. Instead of saying the wind and hail deductible is two percent, it says something like this: if hail damages your roof, you pay the first seven thousand dollars. It explains whether the roof would be paid at full replacement cost or at a depreciated value. It shows how much the premium went up. Then the licensed agent compares the insurance companies that Redbud is able to work with and gives a straight recommendation. Stay where you are, and here is why. Or switch, and here are the exact trade-offs. Or ask your current insurer to fix something, for example to update the age of your roof. And then, with permission, Redbud checks again every year, sixty days before renewal.

The homeowner pays nothing. When a family switches, the insurance company pays Redbud a commission, usually about twelve to fifteen percent of the premium in the first year and about eight to twelve percent on each renewal. On an Oklahoma home policy, after the share taken by the network that gives a new agency access to insurers, that works out to roughly four hundred to six hundred and fifty dollars a year per household.

The long-term vision is to become the household risk advisor for storm country. That means one honest relationship covering home, auto, umbrella, flood and roof, across the eight hail-belt states. And if, and only if, Redbud's own data someday shows that its homes have fewer and smaller losses than average, Redbud could add an underwriting layer, a managing general agency or a reciprocal exchange, that insures verified, well-roofed homes directly. That is a possibility for later, not a promise.

Why you, specifically?

You have already built an out-the-door car price app whose whole purpose is to show people the true all-in cost so nobody gets pressured at the dealership. Redbud is the same instinct applied to the largest recurring household bill in Oklahoma after the mortgage. Your design craft matters enormously here, because insurance documents are nearly unreadable and most of the market is sold by agents who represent a single company and work under sales quotas. You are local. The Oklahoma property and casualty insurance licence requires an exam, not a degree, and it can be earned in about four weeks for well under two hundred dollars. And the brand promise, we will tell you the truth even when it does not pay us, is simply your own value of trust over pressure, written down as a business rule. It also fits your calling to show love to others through business, because what Redbud sells is honest help to families under real financial strain.

Next, the problem in more detail.

About one million and thirty thousand Oklahoma households own their homes, and across the eight hail-belt states the number is roughly fifteen million. In Oklahoma, nearly half of all roofs were hit by severe hail over a recent four-year period, and the average roof replacement cost about seventeen thousand six hundred dollars in twenty twenty-five. Premiums have climbed for years. One reported case saw a deductible jump from about nine hundred dollars to about ten thousand. Insurers increasingly restrict or reprice older roofs. Most families respond in one of three ways. They accept the renewal. They call their agent, who usually represents just one company and cannot offer a competitor. Or they fill out a single online form and get flooded with sales calls. Industry surveys suggest only about seven percent of homeowners shop in a given year, and only about two percent actually switch. Nobody is showing families their bill in plain dollars, and almost nobody proactively re-shops for them every year.

The insight behind Redbud has four parts.

First, in storm country, the money and the pain meet in the insurance bill, not in the roof. Every roof-shaped and contractor-shaped idea died because nobody paid. The insurance commission is a payer that already exists, recurs every year, and rises with premiums.

Second, honesty is the product, not the marketing. An agent who visibly tells families to stay put when staying is right earns referrals that no advertising can buy. Redbud will track and publish how often it tells people to stay. That is something an agent with a single-company sales quota cannot copy without breaking their own business model.

Third, the declarations page is effectively a standard data format that every household already owns. Turning a photo of it into structured information is now cheap and accurate with modern artificial intelligence, with a licensed human checking the result. That lets one licensed person serve many more families than an agency that works by phone calls and scanned documents.

Fourth, placement knowledge compounds. Which insurer will take a fourteen-year-old roof in Edmond this month, and at what price, changes constantly and lives in individual agents' heads. Capturing that knowledge across thousands of placements is the only durable advantage the research found plausible. It becomes meaningful only at around a thousand new policies a year, so it is a later advantage, not an early one.

Why hasn't this already become obvious? Because it is boring, licensed and local. National insurance startups tried to grow with paid advertising and mostly ended up acquired. Local agencies rarely have product and design talent. The combination of a licensed local operator with real product craft is uncommon, and Oklahoma's premium crisis is only a few years old.

That brings us to the question of why now.

Several things have changed at once. Oklahoma premiums are now among the highest in the nation and still rising. Insured losses from severe thunderstorms and hail have exceeded fifty billion dollars nationally for three years in a row. Oklahoma's Attorney General has sued two of the largest insurers over how they handled hail claims. A new Oklahoma law requires insurers to have rate increases reviewed before they take effect, starting on the first of July, twenty twenty-seven. Insurers are narrowing what roofs they will cover. Texas regulators issued a bulletin on the sixteenth of September, twenty twenty-six, pushing insurers to reflect Fortified roofs in their prices. And the technology to read a photo of a policy has become fast and cheap. To be candid, an insurance agency could have been built ten years ago, and many were. What has changed is the size of the pain in Oklahoma, which turned a loyal, sleepy market into an anxious one, and the cost of software that lets a small agency feel like a much larger one. That is enough for a better agency. It is not, by itself, a new category, and the plan says so plainly.

Now let us talk about the first customer.

The first customer is an Edmond or north Oklahoma City household, living in a home built roughly between nineteen ninety-five and twenty fifteen, whose most recent renewal went up by fifteen percent or more, who has not successfully shopped around, and who can be reached through a warm local connection. The decision-maker is typically between thirty-five and sixty, paying roughly four to seven thousand dollars a year for home insurance, often with a wind and hail deductible of one or two percent that they cannot state in dollars. The trigger is the renewal notice, which arrives thirty to sixty days before the policy expires. The usual objections are, agents are salesmen, and I have been with my insurer for twenty years. The second wave of customers will be home buyers at closing, reached through realtors and loan officers, because a buyer cannot close without proof of insurance.

How does Redbud make money, exactly?

Redbud earns commissions from insurance companies. It charges the homeowner nothing. A separate advocacy fee was considered and set aside until a lawyer confirms whether Oklahoma allows it for this use. Redbud will not pay realtors or roofers for referrals that depend on a sale, because Oklahoma's anti-rebating law and its rule against paying commissions to unlicensed people forbid it. In the base case, each household contributes about four hundred and ten dollars in its first year if it has only home insurance, and about five hundred and thirty if it bundles home and auto. Renewal years contribute about three hundred and forty to four hundred and thirty dollars. The lifetime value of a household is roughly sixteen hundred dollars for home only, and about twenty-four hundred for a bundled household that stays at a ninety percent annual retention rate. The cost of winning a household through warm and partner channels should pay back in roughly five to ten months. Through paid online leads, it would take more like thirteen to seventeen months, which is why Redbud avoids buying leads. All of these numbers are estimates until they are measured.

What about market size?

Starting from the top, eight hail-belt states pay about thirty-six point six billion dollars a year in homeowners premiums. At an average commission of around eleven percent, that is roughly four billion dollars a year in homeowners commissions across every channel, before counting auto. The portion realistically contestable by independent agencies in Oklahoma, Texas and Kansas over the next three years is estimated at one hundred and fifty to four hundred million dollars a year. But the number that matters for planning is built from the bottom up: households multiplied by revenue per household. In the base case, Redbud has about eleven hundred households and about seven hundred and twenty thousand dollars of revenue in year three, and about three thousand households and about two point two million dollars of revenue in year five. In a funded, three-state case, it reaches about twenty thousand households and roughly sixteen million dollars of revenue in year five. The market is not the constraint. The constraints are the cost of winning each household, access to insurers, and how often Redbud can genuinely find a better option.

Now, the competition.

The biggest competitors are the companies with their own captive agents, such as State Farm, Allstate, Farmers, Oklahoma Farm Bureau and Shelter, which together write roughly sixty percent of Oklahoma homes. They have strong brands and local offices, but each can only sell its own policy. Then there are hundreds of local independent agencies. They can compare several insurers, but they mostly work by phone and paperwork and rarely re-shop proactively. Goosehead, a large franchise agency, already operates in Oklahoma. It earned about three hundred and sixty-five million dollars in revenue in twenty twenty-five and keeps about eighty-six percent of its customers each year. Digital agencies and comparison sites have reach, but they tend to sell leads to the highest bidder, and several have ended up acquired rather than becoming lasting companies. Newer insurers that work through independent agents, such as Openly, are more likely to be partners than competitors. And the substitutes are doing nothing, calling your current insurer, or raising your deductible. Redbud's differentiation is honesty you can measure, explanation in plain dollars, proactive yearly re-shopping, and product-grade intake that lets each licensed person serve more families. None of those alone is a fortress. Together they make a local brand that is hard to copy for anyone whose incentives require selling a single company's policy.

Which brings us to the moat, and the honest answer is that there is no moat on day one. Over time, several layers can form. In the first year, the book of renewals becomes sticky, recurring revenue. In years one and two, a referral network of realtors, loan officers and, carefully, roofers grows. In years one through three, the brand becomes known as the honest one, backed by a published rate of how often Redbud tells people to stay. In years three to five, at roughly a thousand new policies a year, placement knowledge becomes a real data advantage that lowers costs and raises success rates. And beyond year five, only if the data supports it, an underwriting layer is where the largest insurance distribution companies have found their highest valuations. The plan explicitly rejects weak claims such as first-mover advantage, artificial intelligence, or better user experience as moats on their own.

How will Redbud find customers?

The first ten come from the thirty research interviews. These are households who had a big renewal increase, asked for help, and can be met in person with a printed plain-dollar summary. The first hundred come from neighbourhood groups in Edmond, Deer Creek, Oakdale, Piedmont and Mustang, from an educational evening in the church community, from a clear local explainer page called, why did my Oklahoma home insurance go up, and from three to eight realtors and loan officers. That evening is about education only, never selling at the event, with a sign-up sheet for anyone who wants a follow-up. The first thousand come from a second licensed agent focused on realtors and lenders, from renewal-season content every spring, from a home-buyer checklist shared with lenders, and from a carefully designed, lawyer-approved new-roof review offered through good roofers. The first ten thousand come from expanding to Tulsa, then to the Dallas suburbs and Wichita, with a service centre of licensed customer service staff running on Redbud's software. Distribution should get cheaper with scale, because retained families refer others, partner networks compound, the brand appears in local news every storm season, and placement data raises success rates.

Now let us look at what gets built first.

The smallest experiment that proves the business is not an app. It is a concierge service: a simple web page, an intake form, hand-made plain-dollar summaries, and twenty real re-shops done by a licensed partner agent while you earn your own licence. There is no app until thirty files have been done by hand and the conversion rate is known. After that, the first real version is called Renewal Check. It is an iPhone app and a matching website. You scan the policy with the phone's document scanner, the software drafts the extraction, a human verifies every field, and you see a plain-dollar summary and a licensed recommendation. Behind it sits an agent console showing the queue of files, the extracted data, notes, recommendation templates, the hand-off to quoting software, and a complete compliance log. Redbud buys the commodity pieces rather than building them: agency management software, comparative quoting software, electronic signatures, the insurer networks, and errors and omissions insurance. The artificial intelligence model is treated as a commodity that can be swapped out, and customer data is not used to train it. The first hundred days should cost roughly three to six thousand dollars in cash.

Next, the unit economics and the financial model in plain terms.

The planning assumptions are a five thousand four hundred dollar average home premium, a thirteen percent first-year commission and a ten percent renewal commission, and Redbud keeping seventy-five to eighty percent of commissions at first, rising toward ninety percent once it earns direct relationships with insurers. Servicing costs about one hundred and fifty dollars per policy per year, the target cost to win a household is no more than four hundred dollars, and the retention target is eighty-five percent per year. In the base case, that gives a lifetime value of about twenty-two hundred dollars per household, roughly six times the cost of acquiring it, with a payback of about nine months and a gross margin of around seventy percent after servicing.

The model has three scenarios. In the conservative case, Redbud stays in Oklahoma, pays five hundred and fifty dollars to win each household, and keeps eighty percent of them each year. It reaches about sixteen hundred households and one point one million dollars of revenue by year five, roughly at break-even, and it never pays back an expansion. That is why expansion is gated on measured costs. In the base case, Redbud reaches about three thousand households and two point two million dollars of revenue in year five, with close to half a million dollars of annual operating profit, turning profitable in year four. In the aggressive case, which requires outside funding and a strong co-founder, Redbud expands to Texas and Kansas by year three and reaches about twenty thousand households and about sixteen million dollars of revenue in year five. A separate analysis estimated that the base case needs a peak of roughly two hundred and forty thousand dollars of cash, including modest founder pay, so it is wise to plan for two hundred seventy-five to three hundred thousand.

Here is the scale path, stage by stage.

At one million dollars of revenue, Redbud serves about two thousand households across Oklahoma City and Tulsa, with six to eight people, and the bottleneck is licensed service capacity. At ten million, it serves about eighteen to twenty thousand households across Oklahoma, Texas and Kansas, with fifty to seventy people and perhaps a seed round of three to five million dollars. There the bottleneck is insurer appetite across states and hiring licensed staff. At fifty million, it serves about ninety thousand households across eight states, adding partnerships with lenders and builders, with the quality of service at scale as the bottleneck. At one hundred million, it places roughly one billion dollars of premium for about one hundred and seventy-five thousand households, likely with acquisitions of local agencies, and the bottleneck becomes integrating those acquisitions. Beyond that, at five hundred million or a billion dollars of revenue, the business would require an underwriting layer built on proven loss data. No agency gets there on commissions alone.

So what could the company be worth?

Insurance distribution is valued on operating profit. Agency acquisitions typically happen at roughly eight to sixteen times operating profit, and Goosehead has traded at roughly fourteen to twenty-one times, with a market value of about one point six to two point four billion dollars during twenty twenty-six. A lifestyle outcome, a good local agency, might be worth one and a half to five million dollars. A good outcome, with ten to twenty million of revenue and a repeatable multi-city playbook, might be worth thirty-five to sixty million. A great outcome, with more than one hundred million of revenue across eight states, might be worth three hundred and fifty to five hundred and fifty million. A multi-billion-dollar outcome would require either about two hundred and fifty million dollars of agency revenue or an underwriting layer. For comparison, the flood specialist Neptune went public in October twenty twenty-five at roughly two point eight billion dollars on about one hundred and sixty million dollars of revenue. And ten billion dollars is not conceivable for this model. The plan says so plainly. The final investor analyst estimated the chances of reaching one million, ten million and one hundred million dollars of revenue at about twelve percent, three percent and two tenths of one percent as things stand. With an experienced licensed co-founder, those chances rise to roughly thirty to thirty-five percent, eight to ten percent, and half a percent to one percent.

Now imagine the competition notices.

If State Farm or Allstate notice, they cannot sell a rival's policy, though they may cut prices to keep loyal customers. Redbud's honest advice to ask your insurer to fix something still earns trust, and Redbud wins on breadth and candour. If Goosehead pushes harder in Oklahoma, Redbud competes on local trust, plain-dollar explanation and proactive re-shopping. Google tried an insurance comparison service in the United States and shut it down years ago, and the large technology companies have generally avoided the risks of licensed insurance sales. If artificial intelligence assistants from companies like OpenAI or Anthropic start shopping insurance for consumers, the part about reading the policy becomes a commodity. That is fine, because Redbud uses the same models, and the licence, the insurer relationships, the accountability and the placement data are not commodities. Redbud should aim to be the licensed agency those assistants hand people to. If a startup raises one hundred million dollars for digital home insurance, the price of online leads rises, so Redbud avoids paid lead auctions and builds referral-led growth with regional depth. If roofing software companies add insurance referrals, little is lost, because the roofer channel is secondary by design. If insurers cut commissions, Redbud leans on bundling, retention and performance bonuses. If premiums fall, there is less urgency to shop, so the book and service relationships carry the business. And if regulation slows rate increases, that is good for families, and Redbud explains rate filings in plain language.

Now the failure memo, the ways this could go wrong.

The ten most likely reasons Redbud could fail are these. First, the market may already be efficiently priced, so re-shopping rarely beats the current policy for older, hail-exposed roofs. That is the central question of the whole plan. Second, a brand-new agency may not be able to get insurers that will write older Oklahoma roofs at a normal commission. Third, no experienced licensed co-founder may be willing to join, and you alone would lack the credibility, the existing customers and the time. Fourth, your attention may collapse under finals, an internship, your other projects, and storm weeks when customers need help with claims. Fifth, the cost of winning each household may creep up if organic channels stall and paid leads cost six hundred dollars or more per policy. Sixth, insurers may suddenly stop writing new business after a bad hail season. Seventh, a compliance mistake, such as an improper inducement, an unlicensed quote, or an exaggerated advertising claim. Eighth, retention may fall below eighty percent as families leave whenever someone cheaper calls. Ninth, the agency may half-work forever, earning thirty thousand dollars a year and quietly consuming two years. And tenth, the network that provides insurer access may own the book of customers, so that leaving it means losing the renewals.

The most dangerous assumption is that a new independent agency can materially beat renewal-shock homeowners' current policies often enough, meaning in at least three of every ten cases. The most dangerous competitor is the current insurer's own retention desk, which can reprice a loyal customer the moment they threaten to leave. The most dangerous technological development is consumer artificial intelligence that shops insurance directly with insurers and bypasses agencies. The biggest regulatory risk is that anti-rebating rules limit partner channels. The biggest distribution risk is that partners will not introduce Redbud without payment. The biggest capital risk is expanding on conservative-case costs. The biggest customer adoption risk is that people simply do not trust insurance agents. The most likely reason an investor would say no is that agency economics are not venture-shaped and the team has no distribution edge. And the most likely reason a family would say no is inertia, and loyalty to the insurer that just paid for their roof. The encouraging news is that the most important of these risks can be tested within thirty to one hundred days.

Here is how the thesis gets validated.

The plan sets out ten pre-registered experiments, each with a pass mark and a failure mark decided in advance, so nobody can reinterpret the results afterwards. Three matter most. The first asks whether at least three insurers will write ten- to twenty-year-old Oklahoma roofs through Redbud at a new-business commission of at least ten percent after the network's share. That must be confirmed in writing. The second asks whether, in at least thirty percent of twenty real re-shops, Redbud can find either a saving of fifteen percent or more, or materially better coverage at the same or a lower price. The third asks whether at least forty percent of interviewed homeowners had an increase of fifteen percent or more that they had not resolved.

Now, the first thirty days, sized for about ten to fifteen hours a week around your coursework.

In week one, the goal is to start learning and to open the question of insurer access. Post a neighbourhood research message that makes clear you are not selling anything, and book ten interviews. Contact three independent agents. Put up a simple one-page site with an intake form, but no quoting. Book the Oklahoma property and casualty licence exam for the end of week three. And send inquiries to the agency networks Smart Choice and the Strategic Independent Agents Alliance, and to the insurer Openly, asking for the list of insurers writing Oklahoma homes and their commission schedules.

In week two, the goal is to understand the customer. Complete ten interviews, writing each one up within twenty-four hours, and email eight realtors and loan officers. Design the hand-made plain-dollar summary template, because that one page is the brand. Spend about six hours studying for the exam.

In week three, the goal is to secure a licensed partner and real files. Complete ten more interviews and recruit one licensed partner agent for a thirty-day pilot. Add secure upload and a compliance log. Take the exam at the end of the week, and ask a lawyer for an opinion on how partner introductions can be designed legally. By the end of the week you want a signed partner and at least five declarations pages shared with consent. If no licensed agent will partner, extend the search by two weeks, then reconsider.

In week four, the goal is to decide. Gather the insurer access results in writing. Complete twenty real re-shops through the partner. Speak with three roofers as a small probe of the new-roof idea. Then write a day-thirty decision memo. The plan continues only if three things are true: at least forty percent of interviewees had an unresolved big increase, at least three insurers are accessible at a fair commission, and at least thirty percent of re-shops found a materially better option. If fewer than twenty percent had an unresolved increase, or only one insurer is accessible or commissions fall below eight percent, or fewer than fifteen percent of re-shops found a better option, the agency thesis is killed. The whole test costs roughly one hundred to three hundred dollars and thirty to forty hours.

If the test passes, here is the first hundred days.

On the product side, build Renewal Check for the iPhone and the web, with human-verified extraction, the agent console and renewal reminders. On the customer side, aim for about one hundred and fifty declarations pages, at least fifty insured households, at least three of them from referrals, and a published rate of how often Redbud told people to stay. On revenue, aim to see the first commission statements and at least twenty-five thousand dollars of annualised revenue. On hiring, sign a licensed co-founder with real equity and vesting, and keep a compliance lawyer on part-time retainer. On technology, buy the agency management and quoting software, and automate only the extraction fields that proved stable across the first thirty manual files. On distribution, aim for at least eight partners making real introductions and one local media explainer. On capital, raise nothing unless the day-one-hundred milestones are met. If they are, consider a pre-seed round of five hundred thousand to one million dollars. On partnerships, sign with an agency network only after reading who owns the book of customers, get appointed with Openly, and build a relationship with one credit union or lender mortgage team. And on legal matters, complete the agency licence and errors and omissions insurance, then add a privacy notice, an advertising review, and the lawyer's opinion on partner design.

Then, the three-year roadmap.

Year one is about product-market fit. Expect fifty to one hundred and fifty thousand dollars of revenue, one hundred and twenty to two hundred and fifty households, and a team of two or three: you, a licensed co-founder, and one customer service representative. Build the Renewal Check app and the agent console, and grow through your warm network and first partners, bootstrapped on perhaps five to thirty thousand dollars. The major risks are that the central question fails or that no co-founder joins, so the strategic priority is to prove the better-option rate and the conversion rate.

Year two is about repeatable growth. Expect two hundred and fifty to seven hundred thousand dollars of revenue, about four hundred and fifty to eleven hundred households, and a team of three to six. Add auto bundling, a closing-desk flow for realtors and lenders, and the first version of the placement data model. Scale the realtor and lender channel in Oklahoma City and enter Tulsa, perhaps with an optional pre-seed round. The risks are acquisition cost and insurer appetite, so the priority is to prove a cost of no more than three hundred and fifty dollars per household with at least eighty-five percent retention.

Year three is about scale and regional leadership. Expect seven hundred thousand to two and a half million dollars of revenue, about eleven hundred to thirty-five hundred households, and a team of six to twelve. Let placement intelligence drive the choice of insurer, and build service-centre tooling. Enter Texas and Kansas metros only if acquisition cost and retention meet their targets, possibly with a seed round of three to five million dollars. The risks are multi-state licensing, insurer appetite and service quality, so the priority is to prove the playbook travels. Growth will not be a straight line. Storm seasons and renewal cycles make revenue lumpy, and the plan expects at least one bad hail spring with insurers pulling back.

Now, the founding team.

You would be the chief executive, responsible for product, design and engineering, a licensed agent yourself, and the keeper of the honesty standard. The critical co-founder is an experienced licensed Oklahoma property and casualty agent with five to fifteen years of personal-lines experience, ideally holding a Certified Insurance Service Representative or Certified Insurance Counselor designation, with real relationships with insurers. Ideally this is someone who left a single-company agency because they disliked the sales pressure. They would be the agent of record, own insurer access, service and compliance, and work part-time during the pilot and full-time at about two hundred and fifty households or once funding arrives. Every analyst who examined the plan named this person as the single change that most improves the odds. The first hires are a licensed customer service representative at around one hundred fifty to two hundred fifty households, a second licensed agent for the realtor and lender channel at around three hundred thousand dollars of revenue, outsourced compliance counsel from day one, and a senior engineer for the data layer after a pre-seed round. There are no executive titles until revenue justifies them.

As for capital, bootstrap first, because the test and the first hundred days cost only about three to six thousand dollars. Consider a pre-seed round of five hundred thousand to one million dollars, giving up roughly ten to fifteen percent of the company, only after the day-one-hundred milestones. It would fund a co-founder salary, a customer service representative and the realtor channel. Consider a seed round of three to five million dollars only for multi-state expansion, and only with measured costs and retention. And raise for an underwriting layer only with Redbud's own loss data, never on hope. Without any venture money, Redbud can still become a profitable Oklahoma agency with one to two million dollars of revenue. That preserves every option, including selling the book or raising later from a position of strength. Venture capital is not automatically desirable here.

There are six metrics that tell you whether the company is working. The better-option rate is how often Redbud finds something materially better. The conversion rate is how many recommended switches actually happen. The blended cost of winning a household counts your own hours. Then come twelve-month household retention, revenue per household, and the rate of telling families to stay, which is Redbud's trust metric and should be published.

The kill criteria are written down in advance. At day thirty, kill the agency thesis if fewer than three insurers are accessible at a fair commission, if fewer than fifteen percent of real re-shops found a better option, or if fewer than twenty percent of interviewees had an unresolved big increase. At day one hundred, shelve Redbud as a venture if there are fewer than twenty insured households, if the cost of winning a household is above eight hundred dollars, or if no licensed co-founder or partner will commit. Bring it back if a proven agent offers to join, or if the honest brand starts producing three or more unsolicited referrals a month. At month twelve, if retention is below seventy-five percent or the cost of winning a household stays above five hundred dollars with no sign of falling, stay a small agency and do not expand or raise money. And if Redbud is killed, the pre-registered fallback is the honest-price studio for local trades: trust-first websites with instant estimates and booking for exterior trades.
