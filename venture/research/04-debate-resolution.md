# 04 — Debate Resolution: Where the Analysts Disagreed, and What the Evidence Says

Date: 2026-09-30. Inputs: role analysts A–H (`03-role-analysts/`), plus direct verification searches
run by the lead (sources at the bottom). Protocol: identify each disagreement, find the assumption
driving it, research it, resolve or preserve uncertainty. Agreement among analysts was **not**
accepted as evidence where they shared an unsupported premise.

## Disagreement 1 — Is Section 232 metal content still a live wedge?
- **C (Contrarian):** "Dead." Since Apr 6, 2026, 232 applies to full customs value, so there is nothing to over-value.
- **D (Customer):** "Most acute pain." The 15% metal-content threshold drives exposure.
- **Driving assumption:** whether metal content still affects the duty owed.
- **Verified (A/B, Phillips Lytle, GT, Foley, C.H. Robinson):** derivatives with >15% foreign metal content by weight pay a
  **flat 25% on the entire customs value**. At ≤15% they pay **no 232 duty**, only MFN.
- **Resolution:** C is right that the *valuation* arbitrage died, and wrong about relevance. Metal content moved from a
  proportional input to a **binary cliff: 0% vs 25% of full value**. Evidence of metal weight
  (BOMs, mill certificates, supplier declarations) is now worth more per SKU than before, and it is
  also a mis-declaration risk in both directions. **The wedge survives, reframed from "valuation
  recovery" to "232 applicability determination and evidence".**

## Disagreement 2 — Is a 5-year retrospective audit a viable wedge?
- **Original thesis:** 5-year lookback.
- **C, D and B:** the recoverable window is ~12–18 months.
- **Verified law (A, CBP/19 USC):**
  - PSC is available only before liquidation (≤300 days).
  - A protest must be filed within 180 days of liquidation.
  - A USMCA 1520(d) claim must be made within 1 year of importation.
  - Drawback has a 5-year window, but only for exported or destroyed goods.
- **Resolution:** **the 5-year framing is killed.** Older overpayments are mostly unrecoverable, and
  auditing them surfaces *underpayments* that may require prior disclosure. The retrospective audit
  becomes a **12–18-month in-window scan**, plus an evidence-and-exposure baseline that is sold as
  forward prevention, not as recovery.

## Disagreement 3 — How much is the IEEPA refund opportunity worth to a new entrant?
- **Original:** a door-opener.
- **F and H:** a melting ice cube.
- **A:** ~$44B of IEEPA refunds is still unpaid, and CAPE Phase 3 opens Oct 6, 2026.
- **Verified (A/B, H&K, Morgan Lewis, DWT):**
  - On July 15–17, 2026 the CIT ordered refunds on *finally liquidated* entries **only for
    importers that had sued** (~3,700 cases).
  - Non-litigants depend on the government's appeal or on future litigation.
- **Resolution:** IEEPA fees are not a business. Price competition is at 4–15%, and CBP's CAPE route is
  free. **The IEEPA episode produced something more valuable: proof that refund rights are lost by
  importers who fail to track liquidation dates and protect their position.**

## Disagreement 4 — Is the tariff wall durable enough to build on?
- **Original:** duty moved to "durable statute".
- **C:** each authority is one ruling away from disappearing.
- **Verified (B, CNBC/MLex/Reuters syndication, Sept 30, 2026):**
  - A three-judge CIT panel heard the challenge to the Section 301 forced-labour tariffs today.
  - Coverage reports the court "appeared mostly skeptical" of USTR.
  - The plaintiffs include importers and 25 states.
  - Separately: 301 FL duties are drawback-eligible (B, Alliance CHB), and Tax Foundation projects
    ~$612B of 301 FL revenue over 2026–35 (~$60B/yr) if they survive (B).
- **Resolution:** my original "durable statute" claim is **downgraded from C to D**. The durable fact is
  not the level of tariffs. It is **legal and policy volatility**: authorities have changed more than
  50 times since January 2025 (B), each change creates a new classification, stacking and
  refund-rights event, and every court loss creates a refund wave that rewards importers who
  preserved rights. **The business must be designed to profit from volatility and to survive low
  rates, not to depend on high rates.**

## Disagreement 5 — Should the company be a licensed broker from day one?
- **Original:** yes.
- **C, E, H and F:** partner first.
- **F:** wants a licensed broker on the team.
- **Evidence:**
  - The April 2026 exam pass rate was 22% (A).
  - A corporate licence takes 6–12+ months (C).
  - Caspian already holds a licence and ABI and serves 200+ merchants (A/B, BusinessWire).
  - An importer can grant ACE Reports access to a third party without any licence (B).
- **Resolution:** **there is no licence requirement for the wedge.** The company is broker-neutral
  software plus a licensed broker employed or partnered for sign-off, with its own licence pursued
  in parallel for year 2. This also turns the distribution objection ("you're attacking my broker")
  into a channel: the company works *with* the incumbent broker.

## Disagreement 6 — How big can this get?
- **A:** US mid-market SAM ~$1.2B; SOM $18–35M ARR by years 3–5.
- **F:** base case is a $300–800M exit; a $5B+ outcome requires becoming the system of record.
- **Original scorecard:** $5–15B ceiling.
- **Resolution:** **the original ceiling is revised down.** Honest framing:
  - Base case is a $0.5–2B company.
  - Upside to $10B+ exists only if the company becomes the cross-border compliance system of record
    (the "Avalara of customs"), with a supplier evidence network, transaction-level filing and
    global reach.
  - Probability is low (D, my judgement ≈ 5%).
  - This is stated plainly in the blueprint rather than engineered into the model.

## Disagreement 7 — Who is the first customer?
- **D:** the industrial distributor or manufacturer with Section 232 derivative exposure.
- **Caspian's base:** "merchants" (e-commerce-leaning).
- **Flexport:** freight-bundled customers.
- **Resolution:** D's choice is supported by the resolution of Disagreement 1:
  - The 232 cliff makes BOM-level evidence decisive, which e-commerce-oriented tools do not handle.
  - The CFO has budget.
  - The work resists commoditised classification, because the hard part is evidence, not HTS lookup.
  - **First customer: US manufacturers and distributors, $50–500M revenue, importing metal-bearing
    derivatives and multi-origin components.**

## Net effect on the thesis

| Element | Before | After |
|---|---|---|
| Identity | AI-native licensed broker | Broker-neutral **tariff exposure & refund-rights system of record**; licensed broker on staff/partner; own licence year 2 |
| Wedge | 5-year contingency audit at 25% | 12–18-month in-window scan + **232 cliff evidence** + **refund-rights ledger** (liquidation/protest/litigation tracking), SaaS + capped success fee (10–15%) |
| First customer | Any mid-market importer | Industrial manufacturers/distributors with 232 derivative + multi-origin exposure |
| Moat | Licence + classification AI | Supplier evidence network, outcome data, deadline-bearing workflow, accrual-grade records |
| Ceiling | $5–15B | Base $0.5–2B; $10B+ low-probability path via global compliance network |
| Tariff dependence | High rates | **Volatility**, not level |
